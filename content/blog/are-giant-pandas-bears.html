Yes. Giant pandas are bears. They sit in the family Ursidae, the same family as brown bears and black bears. A raccoon placement, and a family of their own, were real scientific arguments for decades. Genetic evidence closed them. Sources on this page were checked on 20 November 2026.

How the name changed before the family did

The IUCN Bear Specialist Group traces the scientific name to 1869. Père Armand David, a French priest collecting in China, heard about a black and white bear, saw a skin, and obtained two specimens. He named the animal Ursus melanoleucus, Latin for a black and white bear. When the skin and skull reached a museum in Paris, the animal was renamed Ailuropoda, which the specialist group translates as "panda foot", because the specimen looked like the red panda already known to science. The red panda was at the time associated with raccoons. The look of the foot pulled the giant panda toward that argument, even though David had filed it under Ursus, the bear genus. The giant panda guide uses the modern name Ailuropoda melanoleuca.

What the 1985 study actually compared

The argument lasted because anatomy and diet pointed in more than one direction. In 1985 Stephen O'Brien and colleagues published a paper in Nature, "A molecular solution to the riddle of the giant panda's phylogeny". They did not rely on one gene. The abstract describes four independent molecular and genetic measures, used to build one family tree for both the giant panda and the red panda (which the paper calls the lesser panda).

The result splits the two animals apart. Ancestors of the red panda diverged from the New World procyonids, the raccoon family, at about the same time those procyonids diverged from the bears. Ancestors of the giant panda split from the bear line much later, just before the radiation that produced modern bears. The chromosome changes that came with the giant panda's split can be partly reconstructed from bear chromosomes. They cannot be reconstructed from raccoon chromosomes or from the red panda. That is the opposite of what a "secret raccoon" would predict. The paper's own summary is that the dramatic looking differences in chromosomes and anatomy are real but limited, and that molecules and body shape did not change in step.

The Bear Specialist Group now states the conclusion without a hedge: membership in the bear family is supported by abundant genetic evidence and is no longer debated. It puts the split at about 8 million years ago, in the Miocene. San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance lists the same placement, order Carnivora and family Ursidae, and so does the Smithsonian's National Zoo.

The chromosome count that looks like a problem

A giant panda has 42 chromosomes. Ursine bears have 74. A gap that large sounds like a different family. The Bear Specialist Group answers with the Andean bear, also called the spectacled bear, which has 52 chromosomes and is unquestionably a bear. Chromosome number inside the bear family is not a fixed badge. O'Brien's point is the matching one: the giant panda's chromosome pattern is a rearranged bear pattern, not a raccoon pattern.

Eating bamboo does not move an animal out of Carnivora either. The order is an ancestry, not a menu. The taste and gut changes that came with a bamboo diet are a separate story, told in why giant pandas eat bamboo if they are carnivores.

Why they still do not look like other bears

San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance says giant pandas resemble other bears in general build, in how they walk and climb, in skull features, in the social system and in reproduction. The coat is the part that does not match a brown bear in a reader's head. The black and white pattern has its own research, covered in why pandas are black and white. Climbing is ordinary bear behaviour, including for heavy adults: can giant pandas climb trees?

The Bear Specialist Group also recognises three genetic populations inside the single species: the Qinling mountains, the Minshan mountains, and a third, highly fragmented population spread across the Qionglai, Daxiangling, Xiaoxiangling and Liangshan mountains. It says most researchers do not treat the Qinling animals as a separate subspecies. That is a within species argument, not a return of the raccoon debate. The mountains themselves are mapped in where giant pandas live in the wild.
