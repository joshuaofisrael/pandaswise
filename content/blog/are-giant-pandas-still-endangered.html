No. Giant pandas are no longer classed as Endangered. The IUCN Red List moved them to Vulnerable in 2016, and China made the same change on its national list in 2021. But Vulnerable is still a threatened category, meaning a high risk of extinction in the wild, so "no longer endangered" does not mean "safe". The red panda, often confused with it, is still Endangered.

What changed, and when

  • 1980s: the wild population was estimated at about 1,100.
  • 2011 to 2014: China's fourth national survey counted 1,864 wild giant pandas.
  • 2016: the IUCN downlisted the species from Endangered to Vulnerable, citing the larger population, expanding habitat and a stable to increasing trend.
  • July 2021: China's Ministry of Ecology and Environment reclassified the giant panda from endangered to vulnerable nationally, citing more than 1,800 in the wild. This was China's own list, separate from the IUCN.
  • 2021: the Giant Panda National Park was created, merging 73 reserves.
  • 2025: official figures reported nearly 1,900 wild giant pandas and 808 in human care.

What "Vulnerable" means

The IUCN Red List places species into categories based on extinction risk: Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered, then Extinct in the Wild and Extinct. Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered are all "threatened". Moving from Endangered to Vulnerable means the evidence now shows a lower, but still high, risk. It reflects decades of protection working, not the end of the job.

Why the status improved

  • Protected habitat. China began creating panda reserves in the 1960s. Today the Giant Panda National Park covers about 22,000 square kilometres, and officials say about 72 percent of wild pandas are under strict protection.
  • More forest. Official figures put giant panda habitat at about 2.58 million hectares.
  • Less poaching. WWF notes poaching declined after China's 1988 Wildlife Protection Law introduced severe penalties.
  • Connections. Ecological corridors now link habitat for 13 local populations.
  • A captive safety net. Breeding science improved greatly, and the captive population reached 808 by the end of 2025.

Why some scientists still worry

Not everyone welcomed the downgrade without caveats. The main concerns are about the future rather than the count:

  • Fragmentation. Many wild pandas live in small, separated groups. Small populations are more exposed to inbreeding, disease and chance events. Roads, railways and dams continue to cut through habitat.
  • Bamboo die offs. Bamboos flower and die together every few decades. A 2019 study warned that the next widespread flowering poses a major risk, especially to isolated populations.
  • Climate change. Studies project that some key bamboo species, for example in the Qinling Mountains, could decline substantially this century.
  • Slow recovery. A wild female raises at most one cub every two years, so losses take a long time to make up.

And the red panda?

The red panda is listed as Endangered by the IUCN, with fewer than 10,000 estimated in the wild in its 2015 assessment, and the trend is downward. It faces habitat loss, traps, poaching, the illegal pet trade and disease from domestic dogs. If someone tells you "pandas aren't endangered any more", that is true only for the giant panda. Bottom line

The giant panda is a genuine conservation success: numbers are up, habitat is better protected and its status improved from Endangered to Vulnerable. It is still a threatened species whose future depends on keeping its forests connected and protected.
