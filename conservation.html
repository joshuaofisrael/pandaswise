Panda conservation: status, threats and what works

Published 8 October 2026 · Last updated 8 October 2026 · By the PandasWise team

The giant panda is a conservation success that is not finished. Its wild population has grown from about 1,100 in the 1980s to nearly 1,900, and the IUCN downgraded it from Endangered to Vulnerable in 2016. The red panda is heading the other way: it is listed as Endangered, with fewer than 10,000 estimated in the wild and numbers still falling.

"Vulnerable" is still a threatened category on the IUCN Red List. It means the species faces a high risk of extinction in the wild, just a lower one than "Endangered". See are giant pandas still endangered? for what the change does and does not mean.

How the numbers are counted

China's national panda surveys use dense survey lines through panda habitat and analyse droppings and bite marks on bamboo stems to tell individuals apart and estimate population size. Newer monitoring adds fecal DNA and camera images with AI face recognition; Xinhua reported in 2026 that one such system in the Tangjiahe area identified 44 individual pandas in 2025. The next full national survey will update the 1,864 baseline.

What still threatens giant pandas

  • Habitat loss and fragmentation. The Smithsonian calls habitat destruction the biggest threat. Logging and farmland cleared much of the original forest, and roads, railways and dams continue to split what remains, leaving small populations isolated.
  • Small, isolated populations. Small groups are more vulnerable to inbreeding, disease and chance events, and cannot rebound the way large populations do.
  • Bamboo die offs. Bamboos flower and die together over large areas every few decades. Researchers warn the next widespread flowering could hit isolated populations hard.
  • Climate change. Studies project substantial losses of some key bamboo species, for example in the Qinling Mountains, during this century.
  • Accidental snaring. Poaching of pandas has declined sharply since China's 1988 Wildlife Protection Law, but WWF notes pandas can still be caught in snares set for musk deer and other animals.
  • Slow breeding. A wild female raises at most one cub every two years, so losses are replaced slowly.

What has worked

  • Protected areas. China began creating panda reserves in the 1960s. The 2021 Giant Panda National Park merged 73 reserves into about 22,000 square kilometres, and official figures say about 72 percent of wild pandas are now under strict protection.
  • Corridors. Ecological corridors reconnect habitat so separated groups can mix. The national park links the habitats of 13 local populations.
  • More habitat. Official figures say giant panda habitat has grown to about 2.58 million hectares.
  • Breeding science. Captive breeding was once very difficult. San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance and Chinese colleagues developed early pregnancy tests and a cub milk formula that raised nursery cub survival dramatically. The captive population, which official figures put at 808 at the end of 2025, is a hedge against extinction in the wild.
  • International cooperation. Zoos outside China that host pandas do so under cooperation agreements that fund research and conservation in China. See where to see pandas.

Red panda conservation

The IUCN lists the red panda as Endangered. Its 2015 assessment estimated fewer than 10,000 in the wild, roughly half of them in China, with a decreasing trend. Smithsonian researchers estimate a decline of about 40 percent over two decades.

  • Habitat loss and degradation from logging, livestock grazing, firewood collection, farming and expanding settlements. Livestock compete for bamboo, and bamboo regrows poorly in degraded forest.
  • Dogs and disease. Domestic dogs can kill red pandas and spread diseases such as canine distemper.
  • Traps and poaching. Red pandas are caught in traps set for wild pigs and deer, and are poached for pelts; WWF reports red panda fur hats for sale in Bhutan.
  • Illegal pet trade, which the Smithsonian reports has been rising in Asia.
  • Low genetic diversity. A 2020 genomic study found the Himalayan red panda has very low genetic diversity and a high genetic load after repeated bottlenecks.

Red pandas are legally protected in India, Bhutan, China, Nepal and Myanmar. Conservation work includes working with herders and other communities to reduce pressure on forests, alternative income such as yak dung fuel briquettes and ecotourism in Nepal (WWF), wildlife focused ecotrips run by the Red Panda Network, and coordinated breeding programmes in zoos in North America, Europe and Asia.

How you can help

  • Support conservation organisations and accredited zoos that fund field work in panda range countries.
  • If you travel to see pandas, choose responsible operators and follow reserve rules. Our where to see pandas guide lists official sites.
  • Never buy wildlife products, and do not share or reward content that treats red pandas as pets; demand fuels the illegal trade.
  • Share accurate information. Pandas are Vulnerable, not "saved", and red pandas are still declining.

Frequently asked questions

Are giant pandas still endangered?

Not by the IUCN's definition. The IUCN downgraded the giant panda from Endangered to Vulnerable in 2016, and China made the same change on its national list in 2021. Vulnerable still means the species faces a high risk of extinction in the wild.

How many giant pandas are there?

China reported nearly 1,900 wild giant pandas in 2025 data and 808 in captivity at the end of 2025.

Are red pandas endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists red pandas as Endangered, with fewer than 10,000 estimated in the wild in its 2015 assessment and a decreasing trend.

Why do zoos keep pandas?

Zoo and breeding centre populations act as a safety net against extinction and support research. Zoos with giant pandas also fund conservation in China under their cooperation agreements.
